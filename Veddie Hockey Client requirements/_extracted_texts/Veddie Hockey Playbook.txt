1. The business in one minute

Veddie is a premium adult hockey brand for hockey vets who still love the game. It brings the treatment, atmosphere and recognition of a professional hockey experience to players whose careers continue outside “the show.” A vet is defined by a relationship with hockey, not by being old or having played professionally.

The business begins with a spring Champions Cup and summer pickup, then expands into invitationals, recurring leagues and an in-season Champions League. One brand, one D1–D4 classification system, and a recognizable player experience connect every product.

The commercial model combines team entries, individual registrations and recurring playing fees. Sponsorship, merchandise and optional services add revenue as participation grows. The investment thesis is to earn repeat participation through excellent operations, then replicate a proven local model in other cities. 

2. Brand positioning and marketing angles

Brand meaning: Veddie comes from hockey “vet.” It celebrates the experienced player who still wants the room, the rivalry, the big save, the goal and the team. The tone is confident, funny and familiar, with enough polish to feel premium.

Recommended positioning: “The pro hockey experience for the rest of your career.” Master campaign: “Still got it.” Supporting line: “Your game. Your level. Your next chapter.”

3. One division system across every product

Use four skill levels across four age categories: Open (18+), 40+, 50+ and 60+. That creates 16 potential combinations per product. The same labels apply to Cup entries, pickup sessions, invitationals, leagues and Champions League. Each product opens only the combinations with sufficient comparable demand.

Age policy: use inclusive 40+, 50+ and 60+ minimums, with one published eligibility date per competition or season. Open has no upper age limit. Older players may enter younger categories when suitable; younger players cannot enter an older category.

Placement: captain self-assessment plus player histories, current results, strongest skaters, goalie ability and convenor review. A/B/C labels from other leagues are evidence, not automatic equivalencies.

Controls: freeze competition rosters and divisions before play; publish appeal and emergency-goalie rules; prohibit undisclosed ringers. Review pickup placement continuously and permit league adjustments only under a published early-season policy.

Proposed eligibility: former professionals are welcome in D1; active professional players are excluded from the initial recreational offer. Publish any later exhibition exceptions separately.

Examples: Champions Cup — Open D2; Summer Pickup — 40+ D3; Veddie League — 50+ D4. Marketing may display “40+” as “Over 40,” but registration must clearly state the inclusive age rule.

Champions Cup: the launch flagship

Target April or May 2027 between local winter playoffs and summer commitments. Launch region: GTA.

Open expressions of interest across the full 16-division menu. Plan around 16 teams in two viable divisions initially; select the actual age/calibre combinations from applications. Eight teams per division form two pools of four. Each team receives three pool games; the top two per pool progress to semifinals and a final. This is 15 games per division, 30 overall, and up to five games for finalists.

Use one facility where feasible, schedule adequate recovery and publish game length, rest rules and overtime before registration. Expansion requires matching ice, staffing and demand. For a four-team division, three round-robin games each plus a top-two final produces seven games; announce that format and its price explicitly.

Proposed qualification: top 25% of the home division’s regular-season standings, rounded up, plus its playoff champion. A team qualifying twice receives one invitation. Verify the eligible season and final standings. Any inaugural wildcards must be limited, openly labelled and reviewed for calibre. Qualification earns access; current ability determines D1–D4 placement.

Summer pickup: the second launch engine

Start with one dependable weekly session per supported segment from May through August 2027. Cap a standard full-ice session at 20 skaters plus two goalies, subject to session length. Confirm both goalies, maintain a backup list, balance teams and provide a host, clean pinnies, tape, towels, water and music.

Sell individual sessions and capped multi-session passes. Set a cancellation deadline and waitlist process. Offer D1–D4 and age-specific interest lists, but schedule only viable sessions. Capture team interest and captain candidates to build the first league. Do not advertise unlimited memberships against scarce ice capacity.

Leagues and invitationals

Launch invitationals around a reason to attend: a rivalry weekend, an alumni showcase or a well-matched age division. They provide access for teams that have not qualified for the Cup. Keep the Champions Cup’s qualification story intact.

Launch the first league with six to eight teams in one area, on one consistent night, within a supported age/calibre combination. Accept complete teams and individuals; form balanced free-agent teams. Publish the full fee, jersey package, game guarantee and playoff format before payment.

In-season Champions League

Build a cross-league competition alongside teams’ home schedules, using regional knockout rounds and a year-end finals event. Secure round windows and contingent ice first, then confirm each tie after the preceding result. A scalable entry field can feed a Final Eight per viable division; eight is the finals field, not the overall entry cap.

A straight knockout Final Eight requires seven title games. Adding placement games can guarantee three games to each finalist team and requires 12 games in total. Choose and disclose the format before fees are collected. Publish travel boundaries, byes, roster continuity and booking responsibilities.

Initially, the spring Cup is a standalone event and the Champions League is a later product. In a mature calendar, the Cup can become the Champions League’s finals destination. Decide this integration before selling the first Champions League season so teams understand whether they are entering one title pathway or two separate competitions.

Veddie Team Rankings: earn your place

An ongoing public team ranking connects Champions Cup and Champions League results, giving teams a reputation to build beyond a single event. The promise: “Win your league. Prove it against the best. Climb the Veddie rankings.” Rankings become part of the standard experience for both championship products.

Separate leaderboards: rank teams within each age/calibre combination: Open, 40+, 50+ and 60+, each with D1–D4. Start with active regional divisions; do not imply a national ranking until cross-region competition supports one. Never compare D1 and D4 on one competitive ladder.

Eligible results: count verified Champions Cup and Champions League games only. Pickup, invitationals and regular Veddie or partner-league games do not affect the ranking. If the Cup becomes the Champions League finals, each game counts once. Championship titles remain separate honours shown beside the rating.

Proposed launch method: use an opponent-adjusted Elo rating, starting every new team at 1,000. After each eligible game, new rating = old rating + 32 × (actual result − expected result). Expected result = 1 ÷ (1 + 10^((opponent rating − team rating)/400)). Use pregame ratings for both teams; actual result is 1 for a win, 0.5 for a permitted tie and 0 for a loss. Overtime and shootout wins count as wins. Apply the same weight to Cup and Champions League games so teams earn their position through results and opponent strength, without extra points simply for entering more events.

Example: two teams rated 1,000 meet. The winner moves to 1,016 and the loser to 984. Beating a stronger opponent earns more than beating a weaker opponent. Goal margin adds no points, removing a ranking incentive to run up scores. Administrative forfeits and cancelled games affect competition standings under event rules but do not change Elo; completed games with eligibility violations are held or corrected after review.

Credibility controls: label teams provisional until they have played five eligible games; show provisional teams separately from the established leaderboard. At launch, publish a clearly labelled inaugural provisional ranking. Teams with no eligible game in the past 12 months become inactive and leave the active ladder while retaining their history. Ratings carry forward between seasons; archive year-end snapshots and championship honours. Equal ratings share a rank.

Team continuity: maintain a permanent team ID and verified roster history. A name change alone does not reset a rating. Retaining at least 50% of the previous registered roster normally preserves the team record; mergers, major roster changes and moves between age/calibre categories require competition-lead review. Start a new category rating at 1,000, retain historical honours, and never transfer points across categories. Publish these rules before launch.

Player-facing experience: add a Rankings page with age, division and region filters. Show rank, rating, movement since the previous published update, team name, home league, games played, win/loss/tie record, recent form, titles and last result. Team profiles link to verified results and available highlights. Publish updates within 48 hours of results being verified, with a dated methodology and correction route.

Marketing and retention: publish Top 10 graphics where the field supports ten teams, biggest climbers, ranked matchup previews and season-end No. 1 recognition. Campaign angles: “Still got it? Prove it.” “Your team. Your rank. Your reputation.” and “Who is really the best team in your division?” Use rankings for seeding once sufficiently established; qualification and roster eligibility remain separate requirements.

Roadmap and ownership: publish the rules and create team IDs before the inaugural spring 2027 Cup; release the first provisional rankings after that event. Add Champions League results when the product launches. The competition lead owns verification and disputes; the media lead owns ranking releases. Start with a simple results register and website leaderboard, then automate as volume grows. Track ranked-team return rates, profile visits and captain sharing to test whether rankings improve retention.

6. Customer acquisition and retention

League partnerships: invite independent and Plaionn-operated leagues to send qualifying teams. Preserve team identities, feature the home league, offer co-branded content and use a transparent referral arrangement.

Captain acquisition: build a verified captain list, hold short calls, show the exact experience and follow up with a roster-ready registration page. Measure qualified conversations, deposits and paid entries.

Player acquisition: use summer pickup, alumni networks, local hockey creators, arena partners and participant referrals. Require players to opt into future offers; respect partner-league relationships.

Paid media: test small geographically targeted campaigns using real player experiences. Optimize for paid registrations and repeat bookings, not views alone. Do not scale spend before conversion is understood.

Retention: ask for rebooking at the emotional high point after a good skate or championship weekend. Give returning captains priority access, a clear next date and referral credit after a referred team pays.

The repeat loop: a strong game creates a shareable moment; that moment introduces a teammate or friend; pickup gives them a low-friction trial; teams enter events or leagues; returning players generate the next story.

Founding-team offer: a paid, limited first-edition package with priority placement review, a team portrait, founding-team recognition and first access to the following Cup. Protect margins by adding useful benefits rather than discounting everything. A sponsor-funded entry giveaway is an optional acquisition test, not the core pricing model.

7. Revenue model and operating discipline

Primary revenue is payment to play: team tournament fees, individual pickup bookings, season passes and league registrations. Secondary revenue includes committed sponsorship, merchandise, optional team kits and premium services. Include sponsorship in the base budget only when contracted.

Track contribution separately for every product and event: collected revenue less ice, officials, timekeeping, direct staff, athletic support, room amenities, laundry, media, prizes, payment fees and refunds. Then deduct central overhead to understand operating profit. Include founder operating time at a realistic replacement cost.

Break-even entries = fixed event costs divided by (net entry fee less variable cost per team), rounded up. Allocate per-game costs from the actual format and reprice when adding a division changes the game count. Pickup break-even paid skaters = session fixed costs divided by net fee less variable cost per skater, rounded up.

Set a minimum paid-entry deadline before cancellation exposure increases. Use deposits and staged payments with clear refund terms. Keep a cash calendar for arena deposits, staffing, production and customer balances.

Suggested pilot gates, not forecasts: positive direct contribution without uncontracted sponsors; at least 80% paid pickup capacity over six sessions; at least 50% of new pickup customers rebooking within 30 days; and at least 70% of Cup captains expressing intent to return. Validate actual paid renewal separately from stated intent.

9. Team, capital and shareholder priorities

General manager: owns the calendar, budget, arena relationships, delivery and unit economics.

Competition lead: owns classification, rules, schedules, roster checks, officials and discipline. Avoid having sales override eligibility decisions.

Growth and partnerships lead: owns captains, league partners, paid acquisition, sponsors and conversion.

Experience and media lead: owns room setup, towels, music, filming, editing, recognition and delivery deadlines.

Finance and administration: owns payment collection, cash forecasts, refunds, reporting and vendor controls. Early roles may be combined; event-day responsibilities must remain explicit.

Initial capital should fund arena deposits, reusable room and production equipment, registration setup, launch creative, working capital and contingency. Prefer rented or contracted production capacity before buying expensive equipment. Set the funding requirement only after arena, staffing and media quotes; no capital raise amount or valuation is assumed here.

The shareholder dashboard should show paid teams and players, realized price, contribution per game and product, peak cash exposure, customer acquisition cost, repeat booking, confirmed renewals, player satisfaction, incidents and on-time media delivery.

The defensible asset is the combination of trusted operations, reliable ice access, captain and league relationships, credible player classification, repeat participation and a recognizable media brand. Merchandise and sponsorship become more valuable when those foundations work.
